# M1 — Thesis proposal

The first candidature stage: months 1–6, ending at your **M1 — Candidacy** milestone.

!!! note "Who this is for"
    New PhD students and their supervisor. Both sides read the whole page. The *You deliver* sections say what the student produces and how to tell it is good enough; the *I provide / check* and *I hold back from* sections say what the supervisor gives, checks, and refrains from. Treat the supervisor sections as a contract — if I am not keeping to them, say so.

    Assumes [Getting started](../getting-started.md) is done. The clock starts at enrolment; part-time students double every duration. On a three-month clock, see [Compressed timeline](#compressed-timeline-three-months).

## What this stage is actually for

The document is the by-product. The real deliverable of your first six months is a student who can:

1. state the thesis in one sentence,
2. say how that sentence could turn out to be wrong, and
3. has personally run into the main obstacle in the field — not read about it.

A polished proposal without those three is a survey with a timeline attached. Candidacy reviewers test whether you reason like a researcher, not whether the plan will be executed as written. It won't be. The proposal is a contract, not a prophecy.

## Terms — so we mean the same thing

- **Gap** — what the field can't do or doesn't know, *and why it is still open*.
- **Research question** — the gap, narrowed to what this thesis will answer.
- **Hypothesis** — your tentative answer. It must be able to turn out wrong.
- **Thesis statement** — the claim the dissertation will defend: the hypothesis you commit to.
- **Aim** — what the project delivers, in one sentence.
- **Objectives** — the steps to get there. Outcomes with pass criteria, not tasks.
- **Significance** — what changes if the thesis holds.

The gap and the significance are the supervisor's to give. The question and the hypothesis are yours to form.

## Exit criteria — what "done" means

All of these, not most. Paste this list into your proposal's plan issue (see [Where things live](#where-things-live)) and tick them there.

- [ ] Thesis statement in one sentence (template below), plus one line on how it could be wrong. You can defend both without notes.
- [ ] You can tell someone outside the field, in two sentences, what changes if the thesis holds.
- [ ] Objectives (3–5) are risk-ordered: the first is low-risk and uses data and tools we already have. A **fallback thesis** is written down — the weaker claim that still stands if the riskiest objective fails.
- [ ] Each objective maps to a chapter and to the evidence it will produce.
- [ ] A pilot experiment has been run on the weakest assumption, and its result — either way — is in the proposal. If no cheap pilot exists, the proposal says so and the risk rating reflects it.
- [ ] The literature chapter argues toward the gap; a reader could predict the research question before reaching it.
- [ ] The evaluation plan names what evidence would convince an examiner and what result would count as failure.
- [ ] Access to the data / equipment / compute for Objective 1 is confirmed, not assumed. Any ethics approval or data agreement the thesis depends on is identified and the application has started — these take months.
- [ ] You have presented twice to the group — once at a progress meeting, once in a paper-reading hour — and the proposal answers the questions you were asked.
- [ ] The document meets the university's candidacy requirements. <!-- TODO: link to the HDR milestone requirements -->

## Kill criteria — when we stop polishing and change course

- **End of month 2:** no candidate thesis statement survives both tests — "could be wrong" and "decidable by one person in three years with what we have". → First we re-scope the question within the same territory, two more weeks. If that fails too, we change the territory. Wording is never the fix.
- **The pilot shows the weakest assumption fails** and there is no cheap fix. → Pivot before writing, not after.
- **Access, ethics or data agreement for Objective 1 is not confirmed** by end of month 2. → Reorder objectives so the first one uses what we already have.
- **Flag, not kill — by month 3 every edit to the thesis statement has been mine.** That is an ownership problem, not a question problem, and we talk about it directly.

Hitting a kill criterion is not a failure on your part. It is the stage doing its job: finding this out at month 2 costs two months; finding it out in year 2 costs a year.

## Timeline

### Month 1 — Orient

**You deliver**

- The assigned baseline, reproduced. Log in the task issue what broke, what the paper didn't say, and where the numbers don't match. Gaps you find this way are more reliable than the ones in a paper's "future work".
- A one-page *state of the art and where it stops*: who did what, what they couldn't do, and why. Not a list of papers.
- Three candidate research questions, one sentence each, each with "why hasn't this been answered already?" underneath.

**I provide / check**

- **Week 1:** the territory brief — the gap, why it matters, why now; what is fixed by the funding and what is yours to choose; the core reading list (~20 papers, no more); which baseline to reproduce and why that one. I pick one with public code and data, so reproduction takes weeks, not months.
- The weekly 1:1. I read the one-pager and the three questions and comment on the issue within 10 days.

**I hold back from**

- Handing you a finished research question. If I give you that too, you will be able to write the proposal but not to adapt it when it breaks.
- Adding to the reading list once it is set.

### Month 2 — Converge

**You deliver**

- Thesis statement v1, one sentence, using the template below. Revise it weekly and post every version as a comment on the plan issue — the history is useful at candidacy.
- A pilot experiment: take the weakest assumption in your hypothesis and test it the cheapest way you can — simulated or benchmark data is fine. Close it with a `Result:` comment whichever way it went. If it isn't obvious which assumption is weakest, ask; that is a question for the 1:1, not something to guess at.
- Objectives v1 (3–5), risk-ordered, each with an "achieved when …" line, plus the fallback thesis.
- **Group talk #1** (end of month 2): your ten minutes at the fortnightly progress meeting — thesis statement plus pilot result. The audience doesn't share my assumptions, which makes them closer to real examiners than I am.
- A go/no-go meeting with me at the end of the month.

**I provide / check**

- Examiner mode on every version of the thesis statement: how could this be wrong, who has already done this, does the data exist. I say which test a version failed, and I say explicitly when one passes — you should hear "this one passes" before the month ends. If you haven't, that is itself the go/no-go input.
- Which assumption the pilot should test, if it isn't obvious.
- The risk ordering of your objectives and the fallback thesis. This is my responsibility, not yours — in your first year you cannot yet see which parts are risky.
- Confirmation of data / equipment / compute access for Objective 1, and whether ethics or a data agreement is needed.
- The go/no-go decision, recorded on the plan issue (template below).

**I hold back from**

- Letting the sentence drift for weeks without a verdict.
- Expanding scope. A proposal's risk is almost never that it is too narrow.

### Months 3–4 — Write

**You deliver**

- The full draft, written in this order: thesis statement → objectives → gap and literature (an argument, not a survey) → methods and evaluation plan → significance → introduction last. The literature chapter cannot be written until you know what it has to argue toward.
- An evaluation plan that says what would convince an examiner and what would count as failure.
- A timeline with one milestone per objective, and the chapter each objective feeds.
- Sections to me as they are finished; full draft by end of month 4.

**I provide / check**

- Comments on structure and argument, section by section as they arrive, within 10 days.
- Two end-to-end reads — the full draft and the revised one — each returned within 10 days. After that I check only against the exit criteria.

**I hold back from**

- Rewriting your sentences. I will tell you a paragraph doesn't do its job; I won't write it for you.
- "You could also…"

### Months 5–6 — Polish and defend

**You deliver**

- **Group talk #2:** a paper-reading hour is yours — twenty minutes of proposal talk, the rest questions — run as a mock candidacy.
- Revisions from the mock and from the second reader; submission through the university process.
- Your thesis statement in the README of your repo. Update it whenever it changes — it will.

**I provide / check**

- A mock examination: thirty minutes, examiner mode, closed-book — no notes, no AI, no coaching during.
- A second reader (co-supervisor or a senior student).
- Sign-off against the exit criteria above — not against page count.

## Using AI tools

The group rules are on [Using AI](../ai-claude-code.md). For this stage specifically: use AI for anything — search, summaries, drafts, and especially critique of your own argument. Three things must be yours without it — the thesis statement and how it could be wrong, the one-pager's judgement of where the field stops, and first-hand knowledge of the obstacle. The mock and the candidacy are live and closed-book, and that is what they test. Declare AI use as the university requires.

## Compressed timeline — three months

Orient 3 weeks · Converge 4 weeks · Write 4 weeks · Polish 1 week. One group talk instead of two: a paper-reading hour near the end of week 7, run as the mock. Nothing else is dropped: the baseline, the pilot and the go/no-go stay.

## Templates

**Thesis statement**

> This thesis argues that [claim] can be achieved by [approach] under [conditions], as shown by [evidence we will produce].
> It would be wrong if [weakest assumption].
> Fallback: if that assumption fails, this thesis instead argues that [weaker claim], on Objectives 1–[N].

A statement that fills the first line but has nothing to put in the second is not a thesis yet. It is a project.

**Objective**

> Objective N — [outcome state, not an action]. Achieved when [measurable criterion]. Risk: low / medium / high. Depends on: [data / access / prior objective]. Feeds: Chapter [X].

"Train a model" is a task. "Localise damage to within one span using healthy-state data only" is an objective.

**One-pager (month 1)**

1. The problem, in two sentences.
2. The main lines of attack so far (usually two to four), one paragraph each: what they achieve, where they stop, why.
3. What has changed recently that makes a new attack possible — data, tools, demand.
4. Three candidate questions.

**Go/no-go record** — a comment on the plan issue, in the usual decision form (see [Weekly rhythm](../weekly-rhythm.md#meetings)):

```
Decided at 1:1 (YYYY-MM-DD): go / re-scope / change territory.
Thesis statement version that passed, or the tests it failed.
What changes in the next four weeks.
Access / ethics status.
```

## Common failure modes (both sides)

- Reading for months without a question. Reading without a question has no stopping condition.
- The baseline reproduction turns into a four-month engineering project.
- The pilot quietly becomes a full experiment.
- Seizing the first idea and never testing it; pushback arriving at month 5.
- A question owned by the supervisor: the proposal gets written, but the student can't adapt it when results don't come.
- Objectives written as tasks — "literature review", "collect data", "train models".
- Scope creep. Usually from the supervisor.
- A proposal that is really a list of papers to write.

## Where things live

All of this is the normal system from [How we work](../how-we-work.md) — nothing new to learn.

- **The proposal is a Plan issue** — `[plan] M1 — Thesis proposal`, attached to your **M1 — Candidacy** milestone. Its body carries the exit criteria as a checklist and the work packages below as you open them. Thesis statement versions, review comments and the go/no-go record are comments on it.
- **The baseline reproduction and the one-pager are Task issues.** Definition of done for the baseline: "numbers reproduced, or the discrepancy written up".
- **The pilot is an Experiment issue** — hypothesis and setup up front, closed with a `Result:` comment, whichever way it went.
- **Drafts live in Overleaf**, as papers do; link the Overleaf project from the plan issue.
- **Talk slides:** attach the PDF to a comment on the plan issue.
- **Weekly log:** as usual.
